# Salt and Home Cooking: Lower Blood Pressure, Keep Your Food

"They told me my blood pressure is high and I need to cut back on salt. So no more beans for me?" We hear that question, or something close to it, often in the exam room. Behind it is a real worry: that taking care of yourself means bland food and saying goodbye to grandma's recipes, the Sunday meal, the dishes that bring the family together.

The short answer is no. Most Hispanic home dishes can stay on the menu; what changes is knowing where the salt hides and how much slips into the pot without anyone noticing. In this guide, the medical team at the clinic shares practical ideas, dish by dish, so your home cooking works for your blood pressure instead of against it.

## The salt that never touches the shaker

When people think of salt, they picture the shaker on the table. But a lot of the sodium arrives another way: the bouillon cube dissolved in the soup, the chorizo fried with the eggs, bottled sauce, store-bought bread, canned chiles. None of these taste "very salty" on their own, which is exactly why they go unnoticed.

To put amounts in perspective, the American Heart Association (AHA) recommends [no more than 2,300 milligrams of sodium a day](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sodium/how-much-sodium-should-i-eat-per-day), with an ideal limit of 1,500 mg for most adults, especially those who already have high blood pressure. The same source estimates that one teaspoon of table salt holds about 2,300 mg. Put another way, the whole daily limit fits in a single teaspoon, and that includes everything already inside packaged foods.

Your own target may be different depending on your kidneys, your heart or the work you do (for example, if you sweat heavily outdoors). That number is worth discussing at your checkup.

## Dish by dish: where the sodium is and what to do

### Beans

Beans themselves are excellent food: fiber, protein and potassium. The trouble is usually what goes in with them.

- **Pot beans beat canned.** Cooking from dry beans lets you decide how much salt goes in. Make a big pot on the weekend and freeze it in portions.
- **If you use canned, rinse them.** Draining them and running water over them in a colander washes away part of the salty liquid. Look for cans labeled "low sodium" or "no salt added."
- **Flavor without so much salt.** Garlic, onion, epazote, cumin, bay leaf or a dried chile add plenty of character. Salt at the end, after tasting, not at the start.
- **Watch packaged refried beans** and frying beans in lard with bacon or chorizo: they add salt and fat at the same time.

### Tortillas

A corn tortilla made from masa and water carries little sodium. Store-bought flour tortillas usually include salt and baking powder, which also adds sodium. There's no need to ban them; it helps more to notice how many you eat per meal and to choose corn when you can.

### Consomé, broth and bouillon cubes

This is one of the changes with the biggest payoff. A bouillon cube or powdered consomé packs a lot of salt into a tiny space, and it's easy to drop two or three into a pot of chicken or beef soup.

- Make broth with bones, vegetables and herbs, and let it simmer slowly: the flavor comes on its own.
- If you don't want to quit the cube all at once, use half your usual amount for a few weeks, then less.
- Look for "low sodium" versions and compare labels between two brands: the gap can be large.
- Roasted tomato, onion, garlic, cilantro and a squeeze of lime at the table lift a broth's flavor without extra salt.

### Cured and processed meats

Chorizo, sausage, ham, salami, bacon and dried beef are cured with salt. They don't have to leave the table, but they shouldn't be an everyday thing. A practical idea: treat them as seasoning, not the main dish. A little chorizo to flavor potatoes or beans goes much further than a full serving.

### Salsas and seasonings

Homemade salsa with tomato, tomatillo, chile and onion is a friend: lots of flavor with little salt when you control the amount. Bottled sauces, soy sauce, seasoning sauce and Worcestershire tend to carry a lot of sodium per spoonful. All-in-one seasoning powders almost always list salt first; trade them for plain garlic powder, onion powder, oregano, cumin or paprika with no salt added.

### Canned and ready-made foods

Pickled jalapeños, corn, instant soups, tuna, canned vegetables: all convenient, and many quite salty. Rinsing what you can, picking "no salt added" and choosing fresh or plain frozen vegetables are three habits that add up week after week.

## Reading the label without a headache

The Nutrition Facts panel on every product shows sodium in milligrams per serving. You don't need to memorize numbers; two habits are enough:

- **Compare two brands of the same product** and take home the one with less sodium.
- **Check the serving size.** If the can holds three servings and you eat all of it, the sodium triples.

## Your taste buds can relearn

At first, food with less salt can seem plain. That's normal: your palate is used to more. After a few weeks of cutting back gradually, most people find their old food tastes too salty. That's why easing down little by little works better than changing everything overnight, and why it helps to cook for the whole family instead of making a separate plate for the person with high blood pressure.

Other easy supports: keep the shaker off the table, taste before you salt, and lean on the lime, chile, garlic and fresh herbs that are already part of our kitchens.

## What cooking can't replace

Eating less salt helps many people improve their numbers, but it does not stand in for the treatment you were prescribed. If you already take blood pressure pills, don't stop them or change the dose on your own because you now cook differently or feel fine: talk with the medical team before changing anything.

A word about salt substitutes: many are made with potassium. For some people, especially those with kidney problems or on certain blood pressure or heart medications, too much potassium can be dangerous. Ask before you use one.

## How we support you at the clinic

Through our [diabetes, hypertension and cholesterol care](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas), the medical team at the clinic checks your blood pressure, reviews the medications you take and talks with you about how you eat and work, to put together a realistic plan. [Blood tests](/en/services/examenes-sangre) in our lab let us look at kidney function and the body's salts, which matter when food and treatment are being adjusted. Because high blood pressure affects the heart over the years, an [electrocardiogram](/en/services/electrocardiograma) is sometimes ordered. When the plan includes pills, the order leaves the visit as an [electronic prescription](/en/services/farmacia) sent straight to whichever neighborhood pharmacy you name.

One useful thing to bring to the visit: write down what you eat for three or four days, including broths, salsas and snacks. That list tells us far more than any calculation.

## When it's an emergency

If you have severe chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, one side of the face drooping, weakness in an arm or slurred speech, call 911. Don't drive yourself or come to the clinic: every minute counts.
